6 exercises · Grammar, Vocabulary & Pronunciation

1

Formal or Informal?

In formal writing (emails, reports, presentations), use precise vocabulary — avoid informal intensifiers.

Avoid in formal contexts: way better, a lot, really, big improvement
Use instead: considerably, substantially, greatly, significant improvement, commendable, exceptional

Quick rule: if you'd say it casually to a friend, it's probably informal.
2

Build the Sentence

Adjectives always go before the noun in English — unlike Italian, where they often follow it.
 a beautiful house ✓  ·  a house beautiful ✗

Adverbs of frequency (always, usually, often, sometimes, never) go before the main verb, but after the verb be.
 She always drinks coffee.  ·  She is always late.

Adverbs of manner (quickly, carefully, well) go after the verb and its object.
 She finished the work quickly.

With auxiliary verbs (have, will, can), frequency adverbs go between the auxiliary and the main verb.
 He has never been late.
3

Adjective Order — OSASCOMP

When multiple adjectives come before a noun, English follows a fixed order — OSASCOMP:

Opinion → Size → Age → Shape → Colour → Origin → Material → Purpose

Example: a beautiful (O) small (S) old (A) round (S) brown (C) Italian (O) wooden (M) table
Native speakers feel when the order is wrong, even without knowing the rule.
4

Comparatives — "Much", "Way" & Irregular Forms

Some adjectives have irregular comparatives — they do not use more:
 good → better → best  ·  bad → worse → worst  ·  far → farther / further

Never add more to an irregular comparative — it creates a double comparative:
 more better ✗  ·  more worse ✗  ·  way more better ✗

To intensify a comparative, use:
 much (neutral): much better, much worse
 far (formal/emphatic): far better, far worse
 way (informal only): way better, way worse ✓  ·  way more better ✗
6

"Good" or "Well"?

good → adjective: describes a noun, or follows a linking verb (be, feel, look, seem, taste, smell)
 She is a good teacher.  ·  The food tastes good.  ·  I feel good. (= I feel happy / positive)

well → adverb: modifies a verb, tells us how something is done
 She speaks Italian very well.  ·  He did well on the test.

Special case: feel well / look well can also mean to be in good health.
 I don't feel well today. (= I am not healthy)  ·  You look well! (= You look healthy)
5

Pronunciation — Read Aloud

Read each sentence out loud. Before you start, look up the pronunciation of every highlighted word on wordreference.com — type the word in the search bar and click the speaker icon.

Pay special attention to:
• stress on -ly adverbs — the stress stays on the root word, not on -ly: SPEcifically, BAsically, DIrectly
• stress in longer words — very different from Italian: fruSTRAtion, psychoLOgical, parTIcularly
tricky pronunciation words

Part A  ·  -ly adverbs

She specifically asked to speak directly with the manager.
He explained the rules thoroughly and particularly clearly.
Basically, it is considerably easier once you understand the pattern directly.
Specifically, she wanted the answer directly — not basically summarised.

Part B  ·  Tricky words

Her frustration was particularly visible during the meeting.
The psychological impact of stress is often basically underestimated.
He was thoroughly frustrated and decided to address the issue directly.
Specifically, she asked for a more comfortable and direct conversation.
The improvement was considerably better than expected — basically the best result so far.
She specifically studied the pronunciation of each word, particularly the frustrating ones.
