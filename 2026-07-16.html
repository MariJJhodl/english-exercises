1

Never or Ever?

Ever = at any point in time. Used in questions (Have you ever…?), after superlatives with the present perfect (the best/worst… I’ve ever…), and after words that are already negative (nothing, nobody, hardly) — English does not stack two negatives.

Never = at no point in time. It already means “not ever”, so it replaces not…ever in an affirmative-looking sentence: “I’ve never met her” ✓ (not “I haven’t never met her” ✗).
2

Formal Word Choice

In formal writing (reports, memos, official letters) English prefers Latinate words over everyday ones. New pairs today:

• commence (vs. start)  •  inform (vs. tell)  •  sufficient (vs. enough)  •  prior to (vs. before)
• regarding (vs. about)  •  demonstrate (vs. show)  •  rectify (vs. fix)  •  obtain (vs. get)
3

Phrasal Verbs

New phrasal verbs today, common in everyday and workplace English:

• look into = investigate  •  put up with = tolerate
• get away with = do something wrong without punishment  •  catch up on = do something you fell behind on
• fall behind = fail to keep pace  •  sort out = resolve, organize
• call off = cancel  •  look forward to = anticipate with pleasure
