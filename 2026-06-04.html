Phrasal Verbs

Focus today: the give family and other common phrasal verbs.

• give off = emit (smell, heat, smoke, light)  •  give in = surrender, stop resisting
• give away = reveal a secret / give for free  •  run out of = have no more of something
• take over = assume control  •  come across = find or encounter by chance

Choose the Formal Word

Formal writing avoids colloquial verbs, informal intensifiers, and vague expressions. Each question shows a colloquial sentence (in italics), followed by a formal rewrite with one gap.

New pairs today:
• get → obtain  •  hand in → submit  •  enough → sufficient
• give out → allocate  •  not allow → prohibit  •  keep → retain
• agree → consent  •  before → prior to
4

Verb + to-infinitive or -ing?

Some verbs always take to + infinitive; others always take -ing. The form is fixed — it does not change the meaning.

Always to + infinitive: need, want, expect, plan, promise, decide, manage, hope, refuse, agree
Always -ing: keep, practise, enjoy, avoid, finish, consider, mind, suggest, miss, can’t help

Special case — look forward to + -ing: the word to here is a preposition, not part of the infinitive, so it is followed by the -ing form, not the base verb.
5

Present Perfect vs Past Simple

Present Perfect (have/has + past participle): the exact time is unknown, unimportant, or unfinished — the focus is on the result now or the experience.
Past Simple: there is a specific, finished point in time.

Present Perfect triggers: already, yet, just, ever, never, recently, for (up to now), since, this week/month/year
Past Simple triggers: yesterday, last week/year, in + year, ago, when, on Monday, at that moment

Key rule: once you say when it happened, use Past Simple — even if it feels recent.
6

Past Simple vs Past Continuous

Past Continuous (was/were + -ing): an action in progress at a specific moment in the past — used for background, interrupted actions, or simultaneous activities.
Past Simple: a completed action, a sudden event, or a sequence of events.

Classic pattern: I was reading when the phone rang.  →  ongoing (PC) + interruption (PS)
Also: While she was sleeping, he arrived.

Use Past Simple for: a completed fact, a specific count, a finished habit, a result — not for what was in progress.
